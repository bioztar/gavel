A neutral chair for meetings that overrun · September 2026

The problem

Every one of you was in a meeting this week where the first ten minutes were people working out what the meeting was for.

And then it ran long, and the thing it was called to decide got four minutes at the end, or moved to another meeting.

Opening line, said flat. Do not sell it — the room supplies the evidence from their own week. Pause after it.

Why it never gets fixed

Nobody in the room can fix it. Especially not the most senior person.

The person who overruns is usually the person who called the meeting. There is no authority in the room whose job is the clock.

  • A junior can't interrupt a director to say the agenda has moved on.
  • A peer won't — the social cost lands on them, the benefit is spread across everyone.
  • The chair is the problem when the chair is the one talking.
  • Notes tools arrive too late. A summary tells you the meeting went badly. It was already over.
The gap

Meetings have no neutral party whose only interest is the agenda and the clock. That role has to come from outside the room's hierarchy, or it doesn't work at all.

What Cloture is

A chair that joins the call, holds the floor, and puts the question.

Karen is the chair. She takes the floor before anyone speaks, states the agenda the host filed, times each item, and cuts people off when their time is spent — including the person who booked the meeting.

She does not write the agenda

The host does. She refuses to book a meeting that has none. That refusal is the product.

She holds everyone to it

Rank does not buy extra time. A neutral party can say that; a person in the room cannot.

She acts during, not after

The intervention happens while the meeting can still be saved, not in a recap nobody reads.

The vocabulary is deliberate

Cloture is the parliamentary motion that ends debate and forces the question. A judge decides for you. A chair makes you decide. Cloture is a chair.

Why it isn't a wrapper

The decision to cut someone off is not a model call.

It is ordinary code, running four times a second: who holds the floor, how long they have held it, what the agenda promised. Deterministic, testable, and it fails the same way twice.

LayerWhat it is
The chairing enginePlain code. Floor state, timers, agenda structure, interrupt policy. No model in the loop. 93 tests.
The modelWrites one thing — the sentence she says out loud. Replaceable, deliberately.
The earsThin per-platform adapters. They decide nothing.
Take the model away

She still chairs the meeting. She just does it rudely. That is the whole defensibility argument, and it is the opposite of a prompt in a trenchcoat.

Land this flat, no flourish. It answers the sharpest objection in the room before anyone raises it.

The wedge

The teeth are in the booking flow, not the call.

A browser extension on the calendar people already use. No agenda, no invite. By the time the meeting starts, the hard part has already happened.

Before

The gate at booking. The host writes three points and an ask, or the invite doesn't go out.

During

She chairs. Floor, clock, interrupts, the question put at the end.

After

The minute — what was decided, by whom, against which item. A by-product, not the product.

This is why it is not a feature that can be bolted on later, and why it is not a notes tool. Notes are what you read after the meeting already went badly.

Distribution

The extension installs against Google Calendar with no admin purchase decision. One person on a team can adopt it, and the rest of the team meets the product as a participant in a call rather than as software they were told to install.

Architecture

One brain, thin ears.

She joins as a participant, not as a platform integration. The part that decides never imports a call SDK — so Zoom and Teams are adapters, not rebuilds.

The objections

The four questions, answered.

They askThe answer
"Why isn't this a Google or Zoom feature in a year?" They ship notes, not authority. The gate is at booking, in the calendar — and neutrality is the point. A platform cannot chair against its own customer's boss.
"What's the moat?" The boring half: a deterministic chairing engine behind a frozen seam, with adapters per platform. Not the prompt — the prompt is the replaceable part, on purpose.
"Will people accept being interrupted?" They accept it from the agenda they wrote themselves. That is exactly why she never writes it. Strictness is a dial, set per meeting.
"Isn't it voice detection plus a timer plus text-to-speech?" Yes. That is the claim. The deterministic part is the product; the generated phrasing is the surface.
